## Why a Collage

On the way, I noticed a lot of AI and tech ads: a giant iPhone billboard, "Everyone is a coder." above a crosswalk, and a yellow subway poster telling me "99% of you don't think about CI." Someone had even written "wtf does this mean?!" on it.

![Tech and AI ads seen around the city: an iPhone billboard, an "Everyone is a coder." billboard, and a yellow subway poster about CI with "wtf does this mean?!" written on it](/images/garden/creative-inquiry-evergreen/tech-ads.webp)

AI and tech ads seen along the way

Whether in subway stations, on social media, or in pre-roll video ads, you're constantly met with the same cookie-cutter pitch: "Easy to pick up with zero prior experience," "Anyone can become an architect or developer," "AI handles everything for you." Seeing this over and over doesn't just lead to "aesthetic fatigue." It starts to feel hollow.

Part of it is lazy storytelling and the "low-barrier illusion." Packaging complex tools as something "anyone can master instantly" is the path of least resistance, a marketing tactic designed to trigger either anxiety or instant gratification. But anyone with practical experience knows that faster tools and lower entry barriers don't automatically give someone a sense of aesthetics, the ability to define problems, systems thinking, or patience.

Part of it is that everyone is chasing the same market. When capital and products all crowd into the same trending narrative, ad copy starts to converge. Brands borrow from and calibrate against one another until it becomes a generic, smoothed-out script, often paired with a visual style that looks like it came from a single template.

And part of it is the erasure of real-world friction. Many ads show only the seamless final result and hide the messy reality of trial and error, debugging, hitting roadblocks, and starting over from scratch. Yet it is exactly these moments of friction, the tactile, hands-on struggle, that give a process its substance and humanity, far more than any cold, impersonal slogan.

So in a way, the ads became my inspiration. I wanted to make something that works like a poster, but does the opposite: show the friction instead of hiding it. That's what made me think of a collage: something messy, handmade and full of real moments, where every cut and every piece of thread shows.

## Making the Collage

I took a lot of photos that night, printed them out, and turned them into a collage on cardboard. This was my first time making a collage with real, physical pieces instead of on a screen.

![A collage on cardboard: the Little Italy sign photo in the center, the gift shop sign with tiny paper T-shirts hanging on red thread, friends holding a Peroni and a slice of pizza, string lights, and red paint](/images/garden/creative-inquiry-evergreen/little-italy-collage.jpg)

Collage of the Little Italy field trip, printed photos on cardboard

Working with paper, scissors and thread felt very different from working digitally. I couldn't undo anything, so every cut and placement had to be a decision, and the cardboard's rough texture and torn edges ended up becoming part of the piece.

I also used thread. I hung tiny cut-out T-shirts from the gift shop sign with short pieces of red thread, like they were still on display outside the store. While I was making it, this small detail started to remind me of an Italian salumeria / pork store, where salami and cured meats hang in a row from the ceiling.

![Close-up of the collage: tiny paper T-shirts hanging on red thread under the printed "I ❤ NY & Italian Gifts" sign](/images/garden/creative-inquiry-evergreen/threads.gif)

Tiny T-shirts hanging on red thread, detail of the collage

## Where This Could Go

This field trip was more of an exploration than a plan. Since then I've been thinking a lot about what our creative inquiry could focus on, and three threads keep coming back to me.

### Signs and Lights

Little Italy is full of signs: neon, string lights, light boxes, souvenir banners. Sometimes it feels busy, even too much, but it's also a very iconic NYC tourism vibe. The signs don't just say where you are, they perform "Italy" for visitors.

### Food

Italy is known for its food, and food is everywhere here: pizza, pasta, cannoli, espresso, cured meats. Food is also how our group experienced the neighborhood together. It's probably the most direct way people "consume" Little Italy.

### Next to Chinatown

Little Italy sits right next to Chinatown, and the border between them is blurry. When I searched online, I found that this is actually a classic case in urban studies.

The same few blocks have been home to two completely different immigrant communities, one after the other. It's like a living example of an ethnic enclave's life cycle: forming, growing, declining, and being replaced.

Italian immigrants moved from the bottom of the ladder out to the suburbs over about three generations. Chinatown seems to be going through something similar, as some newer immigrants move to Flushing in Queens and 8th Avenue in Sunset Park, Brooklyn. The two neighborhoods show the same process at different points in time.

There's also the question of how Chinatown grew into Little Italy. Was it a peaceful market process, with landlords willing to rent and Chinese families able to pay? Or was there pressure involved after the mob's influence faded? People online disagree, and that disagreement is a question in itself.

Very few Italian Americans live in Little Italy today. Yet through the Italian American Museum, the Feast of San Gennaro, and restaurants that are over a hundred years old, the neighborhood's "Italian-ness" continues. Is that the community surviving, or turning into a tourist attraction?

That last question connects all three threads for me. The signs, the food and the souvenir shops may be what keeps Little Italy "Italian" today, even as the people who live there change.

## Phase 1: Evergreen (Original Direction)

### Topic & Area of Inquiry

As a team of three coming from 3 different cultures, we began our inquiry by looking at a ubiquitous seasonal icon in New York City: the Christmas tree. As November turns into December, evergreen trees take over the city, from towering commercial spectacles like Rockefeller Center to temporary sidewalk stands on nearly every corner.

![A holiday tree going up in a plaza, still wrapped in construction](/images/garden/creative-inquiry-evergreen/metro-tech.png)

Christmas Tree in Construction @Metro Tech, Nov 14, 2026

We became curious about what actually makes a **"holiday tree"** special, and how different cultures mark winter time, light, and collective gathering. Rather than treating the Christmas tree purely as a pine tree, we are investigating it as a cultural interface and scaffold for accumulation—a vertical structure where individual gestures, light, and wishes gather over time. Through fieldwork across NYC, we want to explore how various cultural winter traditions overlap, contrast, and remix in an urban environment.

### Guiding Questions

To ground our initial fieldwork before jumping into making, we developed some speculative "What If" provocations to push our creative thinking:

- What if the holiday tree represented a different cultural tradition?
- What if the Festival of Lights (Diwali) had a representative "tree" instead of lighting the home?
- What if we staged a micro-holiday festival that remixed all three traditions?


To anchor our Phase 1 desk research, we identified three starting points across the city and art/design practice:

Place(s): NYC Sidewalk Christmas Tree Stands & Rockefeller Center: To observe the commercial spectacle, labor, and temporary street architecture of traditional evergreen trees.

What we are planning to do: **Multi-Sensory & Material Sampling:**

1. Capture 20–30 macro observations (bark textures, LED meshes, plastic netting, lantern folds, light reflections).
2. Smellscape Log: Record dominant scents across all three sites (fir resin, sandalwood/incense, citrus/tea).
3. Trace Observation: Document how New Yorkers leave physical or digital marks on public holiday structures.

### Creative Practice we were proposing

1. Magic Crystal Paper Trees. Paper trees that "bloom" with frost crystals over several hours via capillary action, serving as a key metaphor for how a festive object can grow and transform over time.
2. A combined & Living Tree Interactive Installation.
3. A Puzzle Tree.
4. A scent-based gallery.
